# Design for human understanding

Apply to every technical presentation type. These are practical authoring rules
for attention, orientation, memory, and review, grounded in Pablo's selected example.

## Give the reader a reason and a place to stand

1. Open with what matters to a person, then show a concrete example.
   A reader should understand the problem before learning the machinery.
2. Show the whole system at a useful scale before entering one component.
   Name the current boundary and what the next page will help explain.
3. Bridge each change of abstraction: concept, responsibility, component,
   then contract or code. Do not jump from a compelling picture to raw internals.
4. Follow causal or runtime order when it helps the explanation; a directory
   listing is navigation, not the default narrative order.

## Reduce memory and attention demands

5. Introduce a small group of new ideas at a time and one main relationship per view.
   Split dense diagrams; do not make the reader memorize an unseen previous page.
6. Reuse the same concrete example, terms, symbols, and color meanings.
   Keep a compact overview or boundary reminder when returning from a detail.
7. Put labels next to what they explain. Place a source path and entry symbol
   beside the concept being reviewed so recognition replaces hunting and recall.
8. Use whitespace, hierarchy, and a clear reading direction to direct attention.
   Emphasize the change or decision; avoid competing callouts and decorative clutter.
9. Pair the picture or diagram with a brief explanation. Do not duplicate a long
   paragraph on the visual and in narration. Define unfamiliar terms in context.
10. Use color with text or shape, never as the sole carrier of meaning.
    Maintain contrast and readable text at ordinary viewing width.
11. Let the reader control the pace: vertically scroll, pause, open a source,
    and return without losing their place. Essential meaning must survive export.

## Support understanding and independent judgment

12. Explain why a rule or boundary exists, using an example and a consequence.
13. Carry the example into a concrete value, event, artifact, or proposed contract
    so the reader can connect the model to something they can inspect.
14. Separate observation, interpretation, proposal, and unknown. Show relevant
    failures and limits with the same care as successful results.
15. Give a small review task at a natural stopping point: find the owner, follow
    one input, inspect one contract, or check one piece of evidence.
16. Judge success by whether the reader can explain and verify the system,
    not by polish, slide count, terminology density, or agreement with the author.
